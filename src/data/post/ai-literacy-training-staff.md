Most organisations now have staff using AI every day, and most of those staff taught themselves. Microsoft's 2024 Work Trend Index found that only 39% of AI users had received any training from their employer[^1]. AI literacy training for staff closes that gap, but only if it teaches judgement as well as clicks. A tour of features leaves people knowing where the buttons are and not much else.

This article covers what AI literacy means for staff, what separates good training from a one-off demo, how to sequence it by role and level, and how to tell whether it worked.

## What is AI literacy for staff?

AI literacy is the working knowledge someone needs to use AI well and safely in their own job. It isn't knowing every tool, and it isn't a technical understanding of how models are built. For most staff it comes down to five things:

1. **How AI produces an answer.** It predicts plausible text rather than looking facts up. That one idea explains why it can sound confident and still be wrong.
2. **What it's good and bad at.** Drafting, summarising, and reworking text are strengths. Facts, figures, references, and anything recent need checking.
3. **How to check an output.** Reading critically, verifying claims against a source, and knowing when a task needs a human to do it properly.
4. **Where the hard limits sit.** Which tools are approved, what data must never go into AI, and what your policy says about acknowledging AI use.
5. **How to ask well.** A simple structure for prompts, such as Role, Context, Task, so results are reliable rather than lucky.

Notice that only the last one is about operating a tool. The rest is judgement, and judgement transfers when the tools change.

## Is AI literacy training a legal requirement?

In the EU, yes, in a lighter form than first drafted. Article 4 of the EU AI Act has applied since February 2025. It was amended in 2026 by the Digital Omnibus on AI, and it now requires organisations that provide or use AI systems to take measures to support the AI literacy of their staff, without requiring any particular level for any individual[^2]. No certificate is required; the European Commission suggests keeping an internal record of training[^2].

In the UK there's no equivalent statutory duty. The Act can still reach UK organisations that use AI systems in the EU, so check with your legal team if you operate there. For everyone else, the reasons to train are practical: staff are already using AI, and they're using it without reference to your policy.

## What good AI literacy training looks like

The most common format is a one-hour demo: a presenter shows what a tool can do, staff watch, and everyone goes back to work. It raises awareness. It rarely changes how anyone works.

| A one-off demo             | Training that changes practice                               |
| -------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Shows features             | Starts with a real task from the learner's own job           |
| Staff watch                | Staff do something that works within ten minutes             |
| Generic examples           | Examples built around your policy, tools, and roles          |
| Risks as a list of don'ts  | Hard limits stated clearly, then confident use inside them   |
| Ends when the session ends | Ends with each person naming one task to use it on next week |
| Measures attendance        | Measures what staff can do and how sure they feel            |

The opening matters most. Staff arrive asking how this will help them tomorrow morning. Answer that first, with a hands-on task and a visible result, and the guidance on data and checking lands on a tool they already value. Lead with risk, and staff often decide AI is more trouble than it's worth.

Responsible use belongs inside the skills training, not in a separate module. Our article on [responsible AI in higher education](/responsible-ai-higher-education/) covers what that looks like day to day.

## How to train staff to use AI: sequence it by role and level

AI skills training for employees works best as a path rather than a single event. We use the [AI Use Hierarchy](/ai-use-hierarchy/), a five-level framework, to plan it.

**Everyone: Levels 1 and 2.** Every member of staff needs the five foundations above, applied on everyday tasks (Level 1, Conversational AI), then a reliable prompting habit (Level 2, Structured Prompting). Start here even with confident staff. Self-taught users often have strong habits and gaps in the same places: checking, data, and policy.

**By role, once the foundations are in place.** The same skills look different in different jobs, so the second stage should be role-specific:

- Teaching staff: preparing materials, designing assessment, and supporting students' own AI use
- Researchers: literature searching, data handling, and research integrity
- Professional services staff: process improvement, reporting, and communications
- Managers: leading a team's adoption and deciding what to delegate to AI

**Some staff: Levels 3 and 4.** A smaller group will build reusable assistants (Custom Configuration) and design multi-step workflows (Agents and Workflows). Move a team up once it has prompts it uses every week. Those prompts are the raw material for an assistant.

**Leaders: alongside, not after.** Senior leaders need enough literacy to set direction and make the case for change. If leaders haven't used the tools, staff notice.

Two things keep the path going. Refreshers, because features change every few months. And a place to share what works, so one person's good prompt becomes a team's habit. ## How to tell whether AI literacy training worked

Completion rates tell you staff clicked through. They don't tell you anything changed. Ask three questions instead.

**Can they do it?** Test against the learning outcomes the course set, with tasks rather than recall questions. At higher levels, look at real work: a prompt, an assistant, a workflow.

**Do they feel able to?** Ask staff to rate can-do statements from 1 to 5 before and after, such as "I can decide whether a task is suitable for AI support". Confidence predicts use. Staff who can but don't feel sure won't.

**Will they use it safely?** Give short scenarios: spot the red flags in a prompt, or sort tasks into green, amber, and red. That shows whether staff can apply the policy, not only recite it.

Then look again at three months. Are staff using the approved tools more often? Are prompts and assistants being shared? Does anyone ask for the next level? Those signs tell you training has stuck. Self-reported time saved is worth collecting too, as long as you treat it as an estimate.

## Frequently asked questions

**What does AI literacy mean?**
The knowledge someone needs to use AI well and safely: how it produces answers, what it's good and bad at, how to check outputs, what data to keep out, and how to prompt reliably.

**Is AI literacy training mandatory?**
In the EU, Article 4 of the AI Act requires organisations using AI to take measures to support staff AI literacy. The UK has no equivalent duty, but training is the main way to bring staff use in line with your policy.

**How long does AI literacy training take?**
A foundation can be covered in a short online course or workshop. Lasting change takes longer: a path by role and level, with refreshers as tools change.

**What should AI training for staff include?**
A hands-on task in the first few minutes, examples from staff's own roles, your own policy and approved tools, a simple prompting structure, and scenario practice on data and checking.

**How do you measure AI literacy?**
Test what staff can do against learning outcomes, ask them to rate their confidence before and after, and use scenario questions to check they can apply the policy. A free [AI Capability Check](/health-check/) gives you a starting baseline.
